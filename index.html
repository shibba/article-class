Password Managers, MFA, and Passkeys: How the Three Approaches Work

By J. Marlow  ·  September 9, 2026  ·  7 min read

Almost every security guide tells you to do three things: use a password manager, turn on multi-factor authentication, and switch to passkeys where you can. The three are often listed together as if they were interchangeable. They are not. Each one solves a different part of the problem, and understanding which part is what makes the advice usable.

Why passwords alone keep failing

The weakness of a password is not that it is short. It is that it is a shared secret. You know it, the website knows it, and anything that intercepts it, whether a fake login page or a database breach, now knows it too. Reuse turns one leak into many, because attackers replay stolen pairs of usernames and passwords across other sites.

Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report found that vulnerability exploitation had overtaken stolen credentials as the most common way attackers first get into a network, the first time that has happened in the report's nineteen-year history. Stolen credentials did not stop mattering, though. The same report traced a pipeline in which information-stealing malware harvests credentials that brokers then sell to ransomware crews, and found that half of ransomware victims who had suffered an earlier credential leak were attacked within 95 days of it.

The current federal guidance reflects this shift in thinking. NIST Special Publication 800-63B, revision 4, tells the systems that verify your password to require at least 15 characters when the password is the only factor, and at least 8 when it is paired with a second factor. It also tells them to allow passwords of at least 64 characters, and it is blunt about the rules most of us grew up with:

Verifiers and CSPs SHALL NOT impose other composition rules (e.g., requiring mixtures of different character types) for passwords. Verifiers and CSPs SHALL NOT require subscribers to change passwords periodically.

Rotation is required only when there is evidence that the password has been compromised. What the standard does require is a blocklist check, so that known-breached and obvious passwords are rejected at the moment they are chosen.

Password managers: unique secrets at scale

A password manager solves the reuse problem by making uniqueness free. It generates a long random string for every account, stores it in an encrypted vault, and fills it in when you return. You remember one strong passphrase instead of a hundred weak variations.

Two benefits get less attention than they deserve. The first is that autofill checks the domain before it types anything, so a manager will not hand your bank password to a look-alike site, which makes it a quiet defense against phishing. The second is simply that a manager makes it practical to follow the length guidance: a 30-character random password costs you nothing if you never type it.

NIST is explicit that sites must not get in the way here. Verifiers shall allow the use of password managers and autofill, and should permit pasting into password fields, a reversal of the old habit of blocking paste in the name of security.

The vault is one target holding everything, so the master passphrase should be long and unique, and the vault itself should be protected with a second factor. It is a reasonable trade: one well-defended secret in place of a hundred poorly defended ones.

Multi-factor authentication: not all factors are equal

MFA adds a second proof to the password, and it stops the plain replay of stolen credentials. But the category covers methods that differ enormously in how well they hold up against a live attacker.

CISA's fact sheet on the subject sorts them plainly. SMS and voice codes are "vulnerable to phishing, SS7, and SIM swap attacks" and should be used only as a last resort. Push notifications without number matching are vulnerable to push bombing, where an attacker fires off prompts until a tired user approves one. One-time codes from an authenticator app are better, but they are still a string a person can be talked into reading out on a convincing fake login page.

The agency is equally clear that this is not a reason to skip MFA:

While any form of MFA is better than no MFA and will reduce an organization's attack surface, phishing-resistant MFA is the gold standard.

NIST classifies SMS delivery as a restricted authenticator, meaning verifiers should weigh the risk before relying on it and must offer an alternative.

Passkeys: nothing left to steal

A passkey changes the shape of the problem instead of hardening the secret. When you create one, your device generates a public and private key pair. The site keeps the public key; the private key stays on your device and is never transmitted. Signing in means your device proves it holds the private key, and the browser will only do that for the exact site the key was created for.

That last detail is the whole point. There is no code to read out and no password to type into the wrong box, and a look-alike domain cannot elicit a valid signature. CISA puts it simply: "The only widely available phishing-resistant authentication is FIDO/WebAuthn authentication."

Adoption has moved quickly. The FIDO Alliance reported around five billion passkeys in use by World Passkey Day in May 2026, with 90 percent of surveyed consumers aware of passkeys, 75 percent having enabled one on at least one account, and 49 percent using them regularly where they are offered. Among enterprises surveyed, 68 percent had deployed passkeys for employee sign-in or were in the process of doing so.

Passkeys are not a finished story. Most consumer passkeys sync through a platform account, which makes them convenient and recoverable but ties their security to that account, and NIST does not permit syncable authenticators at its highest assurance level. Account recovery is the softer edge: if the fallback path is an emailed link or an SMS code, that path is now the weakest point.

How the three fit together

They are layers, not competitors. Use passkeys wherever a site offers them, since they remove the shared secret entirely. Where passkeys are not available, use phishing-resistant MFA if you can, and app-based codes rather than SMS if you cannot. Keep a password manager for the long tail of sites that will still want a password for years, let it generate long random ones, and protect the vault itself with a strong passphrase and a second factor. Change a password when there is a reason to, not on a calendar.
